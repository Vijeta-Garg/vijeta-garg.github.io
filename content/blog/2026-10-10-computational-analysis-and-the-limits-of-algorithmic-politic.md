Mamdani’s lassi reel exemplifies that political rhetoric on social media is layered by design, because social media enables politicians to speak to diverse masses with every post. Therefore, politicians use various languages, settings, and features to communicate their points--which are more complex than singular speeches to specific audiences that politicians gave before the digital age. Complex rhetoric requires complex analysis; therefore, researchers and platform moderators often use algorithms to quantify and analyze this rhetoric. Computational algorithms can reveal unique patterns across fragmented rhetoric on social media platforms, because they are excellent at parsing large amounts of data and drawing quantifiable relationships. The patterns they create provide analysts a comprehensive view of politicians’ rhetoric, a view otherwise arduous to manually construct across thousands of posts. Despite their advantages in revealing such patterns, algorithms often struggle to comprehensively analyze layered rhetoric. For example, in the lassi reel, a traditional transcription algorithm2 may misread Mamdani’s Hindi-language words--words which help many South Asian voters comprehend and identify with Mamdani’s campaign. Understanding these algorithms’ limitations remains crucial, because algorithmic misunderstanding of layered political rhetoric biases presentation of political content. Such bias is structural and invisible, but a social media algorithm that misframes or misdirects content can subtly distort civic participation. Understanding the ways these algorithms succeed and fail in rhetorical analysis, then, is essential. Computational analysis techniques reveal patterns in the ways digital political rhetoric connects to diverse audiences; however, nuanced political rhetoric necessitates further human interpretation to prevent distortion of civic discourse.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;</p><p><span id="docs-internal-guid-4f2c34e2-7fff-af44-6950-630c2e03d8ea"></span></p><p>	These patterns emerge because computational analysis can analyze prolific yet fragmented online rhetoric at a scale that humans cannot; however, computational findings still require further human interpretation to convey meaning. For example, the study “Style and Rhetoric of Spanish Politics on Twitter” by Spanish researchers Ceia, et. al reveals rhetorical patterns by analyzing 10,038 tweets about feminism across various political parties. In the study, while the researchers’ standard word frequency analysis supported that each party’s diction reflected their positions on the topic, further analysis via co-occurrence networks3 indicated a more nuanced relationship between ideology and words. Ceia, et. al found the connection between the words mujer (woman) hombre y (man and) using the co-occurrence network graph below in which the width of connecting lines establishes severity of connection.</p><p><span id="docs-internal-guid-917b1246-7fff-8e05-9672-1e2101c53e68"></span></p><span id="docs-internal-guid-917b1246-7fff-8e05-9672-1e2101c53e68"><p><img loading="lazy" src="https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgQ0no22kEE8xRPpl9VnxY-4U0LgT1RkwQ5pjLyZpgxpYVdrEFVQqWvpsipu2dDaWhdygTmrDdBPKvMneqbWL0N17W5J5cVgbqG8zckPfYwEI8087EvG6f-DMcX3dTMeT2dz5ZIyao0_JzSfh1hVvQVOfKNplhvpEkvVvfUGljjD6HXhShi7XOldWe_jY0-/s1600/image.png" />&nbsp;</p><p>Fig 1. (Ceia et. al 15)&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;</p><p>As illustrated by Fig.1, the phrase Hombre y (men and) strongly connects to mujer (woman) across every political party. At first glance, this relationship seems incidental or innocuous. Heteroglossic4 by design, this rhetoric helps politicians connect with diverse audiences at the same time: Wwhile the topic of feminism gains traction with women for obvious reasons, Ceia et. al notes that associating the word mujer to hombre allows parties to reassure men that “the party still sees a place for men within their discussions of women’s rights and feminism” (Ceia et. al 15). In the digital age, tweets retain a wider readership than their intended group, and this digital format causes politicians to qualify their concerns for a specific group of voters by connecting their messages to a “larger whole.” Therefore, by advocating for feminism while mentioning men, these parties convey separate relatable messages to both groups of voters and gain loyalty from a larger voter base. In this instance, while co-occurrence networks made the relationship between these words visible, further human interpretation positioned this pattern as a heteroglossic method used to gain traction with a broad group of voters. If algorithms miss such nuances, they may label this content solely “pro-women,” and amplify this rhetoric only towards women. This misinterpretation could lead to narrowing the target audience in ways that further contribute to polarization and echo chambers. Computers’ struggle analyzing such subtle relationships between words reflects fundamental algorithmic limitations. University of Pennsylvania Professor Lanham’s framework of the “serious man” and “rhetorical man” in his text The Rhetorical Idea of Life diagnoses the reasons behind limitations of algorithms like the ones Ceia. et al employed. In the text he defined the “serious man” as an entity which adheres strictly to the denotations of language. On the other hand, he proclaimed that “ rhetorical man” looks for relationships within language, and understands implications rather than simply denotations (Lanham 2). In Ceia et.al’s study, the co-occurrence networks were the “serious man.” They looked at words and the connections between them, and they quantified language into graphs and data. However, the human interpreters, Ceia et. al, acted as the rhetorical man. These researchers analyzed: Do these words have a relationship beyond the obvious? What can these words tell us about rhetoric and society? Clearly, while the serious man can sift through multitudes of data to reveal hidden connections, the work of the rhetorical man essentially clarifies subtle meaning. Therefore, as Lanham explains, Ceia et. al’s algorithms produced limited analysis because computers struggle with acting like the rhetorical man. Admittedly, some algorithms resemble the rhetorical man more than others; however, they still require human contextualization. For example, sentiment analysis--another technique Ceia et. al employed within their study--represents an algorithm arguably closer to analyzing nuanced analysis of relationships between words: Sentiment analysis quantifies connotations within text, therefore “understanding” meaning better than co-occurrence network algorithms. While using this technique, the researchers in the Spanish politics study observed that Podemos party’s tweets displayed a fair amount of negativity despite its identity as a pro-feminist party. However, upon closer examination, the researchers found the origin of this negativity: “Podemos adopted a strongly negative tone when criticizing the policies of other parties” (Ceia et. al 22). Finding both positive and negative sentiment within the Podemos posts exemplifies that human sentiment is difficult to categorize neatly. In this instance, Podemos attempts to convey positive messages about feminism at the same time as negatively portraying opposition parties, communicating two different messages at the same time. This heteroglossic rhetoric, in part, contributes to computational confusion. Once again, algorithms fail to replicate the rhetorical man as well as humans, because of the presence of subtle relationships between words. This failure has real repercussions: While categorizing the overall sentiment of this text, an algorithm might consider Podemos a more neutral source about feminism than the party actually represents. This algorithmic misframing of content sentiment redirects Podemos content from feminist users&nbsp; towards more neutral users, consequently reducing its impact. Thus, as mirrored by the serious man/rhetoric man framework, computational analysis struggles to parse through context without human guidance. These struggles are only compounded by political rhetoric shifting online.&nbsp;</p><p>Social media, for example,&nbsp; complicates computational analysis by offering numerous features that can communicate different political implications to diverse audiences simultaneously. This multimodality includes features such as likes, follows, hashtags, comments. Ceia et. al analyzed one of these features--specifically, campaign hashtags. Generally, hashtags allow users to find specific content by acting as “blurbs” to attract new eyes. Their ability to pack titular information makes the hashtag feature useful to analyze. However, computational analysis is difficult and often inaccurate with small blurbs like hashtags: Ceia et. al needed to manually count the hashtag frequencies to analyze them. The researchers suggested that this difficulty originated from a lack of computational markers within hashtags, because they found “many hashtags do not have the word ‘women’ in them” and neither are they&nbsp; “recognized in common discourse about women” (Ceia et. al 29). Computational analysis needs markers or recognition patterns to follow, but short hashtags are often mottos or movement names that have sprung up organically and lack these markers. The text “Language is Alive” by Buzzfeed editor Emmy J. Favilla explains the source of Ceia. et al’s difficulties with hashtag analysis by defining language as a fluid reflection of society. Favilla details that online communication has accelerated language change to a pace “never before seen in the history of the English language” (Favilla 14). Favilla’s observation supports that social media has complicated political rhetoric: to connect to their audience, now politicians must integrate various aspects of a rapidly changing language into their reels and posts. As a result, digital political rhetoric also evolves at an unprecedented pace difficult for computers to keep up with. Favilla’s observation therefore explains Ceia. et al’s difficulty with hashtag analysis: computational tools are unable to keep up with a rapidly evolving form of language. As a result, these tools failed to recognize new movements hashtag names derived from or slang they incorporated. Despite their complexity, these features remain imperative to understand: Both Favilla and Ceia et. al use or analyze hashtags to convey new information, reinforcing that hashtags represent an invaluable source of information for analyzing political communication. Ignorance of these media features may cause algorithms to miss important context and amplify messages to the wrong audience. In other words, computational misunderstanding can distort the spread of political rhetoric through one of the most important mediums of thought and communication in the modern world--social media. This ______ is the same powerful medium of communication that Zohran Mamdani, 2025 New York City mayoral candidate, took advantage of as he set a precedent for one of the most successful social media campaigns in recent history.&nbsp;</p><p>	In his disruptive 2025 campaign, Zohran Mamdani used social media at an unprecedented scale to elevate both the communication and mobilization aspects of his campaign, winning votes from the most densely diverse city in the world. His audience incentivized him to lean into the multiculturalism of New York City through his heteroglossic diction and multilingualism. Despite reviews commending his novel means of connecting with his audience, his precedent-setting campaign has been understudied computationally. Applying computational techniques from the Spanish research study to Mamdani’s digital campaign can reveal nuanced strategies within his rhetoric. Furthermore, the Spanish tweet study was conducted in 2019-- before widespread usage and development of large language models. As a result, the study employed simpler tools than the ones open-source today. An extension of these techniques with current technology investigates whether computational analysis has changed from 2019 to now: Do current techniques decrease the need for human contextualization?</p><p>	The Mamdaniator tool extends a well-known web scraping pipeline (similar to Ceia et. al’s study) for such an investigation. This tool analyzes Mamdani’s political communication across diverse voter bases by using a curated dataset of Mamdani’s campaign reel transcripts. Similar to the Spanish research study analyzing Spanish parties’ tweets, Mamdaniator analyzes fragmented representatives of Mamdani’s campaign to find broad patterns defining his rhetoric. Furthermore, Mamdaniator extends the Spanish researchers’ diction and sentiment analysis to Zohran Mamdani’s online campaign. Taking advantage of newer technology, Mamdaniator also examines Mamdani’s usage of multilingualism to connect with diverse audiences. Together these methods reveal both the potentials and limitations of computational analysis of political rhetoric.&nbsp;</p><p>	Applying these methods to Mamdani's reels, Mamdaniator found similar rhetorical patterns to those Ceia et. al uncovered in Spanish political tweets. A closer look at Mamdaniator’s word frequency analysis, as displayed in Fig. 2 below, reveals Mamdani most frequently used variants of the word “New York” across his reels.</p><br /><p><img loading="lazy" src="https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgWHY8OUMDDVX3gtmLoXlenyGmqwMV3rviByhaEkfANu2rBUOmDKrzqqdK28VhGNJYM_gsbi7FShsNfJ-otje3vEUX5uAnKvCX8ziT4reM8mjvkWvwdbaWKzQmItzf4k60OEluNdkQbRmy9CSnIdvKpSH0jsYuIeTwhf3OfFyaCAWud7-zJySmbahUH92RC/s1600/image.png" /></p><p>Fig 2. Mamdaniator Heteroglossic Dashboard (Garg, Github)</p><p>The computational analysis presented in Fig. 2 represents a valuable pattern amongst Mamdani’s fragmented posts: to connect to the diverse community of New York, Mamdani focused on the city as a uniting identity. Regardless of his target audience in a specific video, all of his voters are New Yorkers. Mamdani used this common identity to qualify his concerns for minorities. Even as he reassured the minority he recognizes their specific struggles, he repeated the word “New York” to communicate to the majority that he still prioritized New York as a whole. His strategy extends Ceia et. al’s study by demonstrating that politicians use heteroglossic rhetoric across cultures as well as genders or ideas. In fact, Mamdani’s heteroglossic strategy led internet culture reporters Dylan Wells and Tatum Hunter to describe Mamdani’s campaign as "empathetic and comprehensive” in the Washington Post (Wells and Hunter). Mamdani communicates with specific groups; therefore, he cultivates an “empathetic campaign” in which everyone feels seen. Examples of his authenticity include his lassi post and his many reels at temples, mosques, and churches. At the same time, Mamdani unifies these diverse voters by connecting his concerns for them to the greater whole--New York City. By doing so, he cultivates a "comprehensive" campaign. While both Mamdaniator and Ceia. et al’s study reveal a heteroglossic pattern in digital political rhetoric, Wells and Hunter observe its civic effect and illustrate the reason politicians may use this technique. Significantly, Wells and Hunter’s observations exemplify the limitations of Mamdaniator and Ceia et. al’s computational modeling: Wwhile algorithms can identify techniques politicians use, they fail to identify reasoning and effectiveness of the technique. Once again, computational models struggle with effectively enacting the rhetorical man.</p><p>	The insufficient availability and accuracy of multilingual analysis tools represent further computational limitations in Mamdani’s campaign analysis. Though heteroglossic, the rhetoric in Ceia et al’s study remained monolingual. In contrast, Mamdani’s campaign targeted a multilingual audience through a video format. This format enabled Mamdani to frequently “codeswitch” as he communicated with different voter bases--he explained gas prices in Spanish, recounted the voting system in Hindi, and celebrated Eid in Urdu. However, computational tools’ abilities to document and analyze this multilingual rhetoric are limited due to insufficient development of application programming interfaces (APIs)5 to handle text in various languages. Traditional APIs to analyze text, such as natural language processing (NLP)6 pipelines, tend to return inconsistent responses with input languages other than English. Mamdaniator, for example, significantly undercounts Mamdani’s usage of Spanish because of traditional NLPs’ inaccuracy. Furthermore, as illustrated by Fig. 3 below, the Hindi/Urdu subset of the Mamdaniator dataset returns zero tokens (no response for further analysis from APIs) from a standard, well-developed English NLP pipeline.&nbsp;</p><br /><p><img loading="lazy" src="https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEhNOh8Zmv7hT2KXRDNSY3AVoOXqL5KGVBsFMs59qZ_maeMUZEv_TJL8Kz0Ey3iUEvVB2p3U4aXUkT-NBhyphenhyphenc1ZLFIwE9hUU2JoOUqVCvNZ9CBWL_421G92sCHZW8o1Gd56Iu2EK_T7TbdHXy_5iz_HyuIjvbFdlojuS7EKVl_u1oLHjvMSAu4YopOYjCEXkZ/s1600/image.png" /></p><p>Fig. 3 Mamdaniator Multilingual Dashboard (Garg, Github)&nbsp;</p><p>This lack of tokens indicate the NLP pipeline’s inability to parse Hindi/Urdu text overall. Mamdaniator uses traditional API tools, so these tools’ inability to parse various languages indicate algorithms flatten distinct multilingual connotations. These connotations provide important means of connecting with diverse communities: In her TedX talk “3 Ways to Speak English,” Social Justice Professor Jamila Lysiscott emphasized the contextual importance of language diversity. She codeswitched in her slam poetry, describing herself as a "tri-lingual orator" as she spoke "one tongue for each: home, school, and friends" (Lysiscott). While she didn’t switch between languages in her speech, she communicated distinctly with only minor shifts in her English vocabulary. As she switched between “tongues” Lysiscott even changed her tone and her actions, displaying that the same message needs to be communicated to different communities in different ways to make sense. This phenomenon also applies at a larger scale as politicians such as Mamdani switch between languages. In fact, Mamdani himself articulated the priority he placed on creating authentic connection through multilinguality: he declared his campaign would "not only espouse politics that speaks directly to New Yorkers' lives, but also communicate that politics directly to New Yorkers" (Mamdani qtd. in Wells and Hunter). Mamdani repeats the word “direct” twice, demonstrating the importance he placed on integrating with the citizens’ needs and lives, which he is able to accomplish with codeswitching. While Lysiscott’s Ted Talk illustrates codeswitching as a means for authentic connection, Mamdani’s campaign statement expands the necessity of this codeswitching by demonstrating its importance in a multilingual political context. By highlighting the political importance of multilingual usage, both these sources also emphasize the importance of algorithmic understanding of multilingual rhetoric: Iif political communication in other “codes” is more misframed and misdistributed than discourse in English, algorithms that shape media cultivate a fundamental disconnect between non-English speaking communities and civic participation.&nbsp;</p><p>	Computational tools expose patterns in political usage of heteroglossic rhetoric to connect across diverse audiences. However, gaps in algorithms’ ability to analyze digital rhetorical elements highlight the necessity of further human interpretation. As Zohran Mamdani proclaimed “ मैं यह सब बदलने के लिए लड़ रहा हूं |” 1 in front of that lassi stall, he leveraged digital rhetoric to connect with his multilingual audience. Algorithmic inability to computationally process and analyze his lassi rhetoric, even with the more advanced tools of 2026 as compared to 2019, divulge more than a technical or academic gap. They reveal a hidden bias in political presentation. If algorithms are trained on biased datasets--such as the solely-English NLPs in Mamdaniator--they fail to pick up important political nuances. Therefore, computational limitations cultivate bias in the presentation and propagation of online rhetoric. Spotting this bias is essential to comprehensive civic understanding. In his paper “Computational Propaganda and Big Data,” Oxford Professor Howard stated that treating technical mechanisms as unbiased systems produces a determinism which limits our ability to “argue for change in the social value and meaning of content” (Howard 1). In accordance with his statement, recognizing the algorithmic bias within our technical systems allows us to argue for a more equitable shaping of modern politics and media. Recognizing such algorithmic bias is the foundation for civic participation in a digital age. Making arguments, as Howard encourages us to, require individual human interpretation as illustrated by Lanham’s framework. The serious man--the algorithms--can quantify and store data, but understanding human connection and contextualization remains ours to interpret.
