TL;DR as a descendent of colonized people I make an argue that DEI is central to the progression of society. Who are we but a reflection of opportunities and barriers of those who came before us?</p><p></p><div><a href="https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEi7efN-W_RCuTQBLHdUhMNYOHHtE4Unf0ErnzO75m7_f_5Y5HOi8aCF7cfumLvWWxvRQkIIZonVi12-IpiGZZYpoVINPRXZ2ORqtnMkjMcOnkmCvkN85qY2t8NmYryorkeAeLgb0DL3oSCnI7X2h5slKSFHkeQIg7Fq7-kXZQMtVApsYGn3I0HMbyV1D02N/s1600/image.png"><img loading="lazy" alt="" src="https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEi7efN-W_RCuTQBLHdUhMNYOHHtE4Unf0ErnzO75m7_f_5Y5HOi8aCF7cfumLvWWxvRQkIIZonVi12-IpiGZZYpoVINPRXZ2ORqtnMkjMcOnkmCvkN85qY2t8NmYryorkeAeLgb0DL3oSCnI7X2h5slKSFHkeQIg7Fq7-kXZQMtVApsYGn3I0HMbyV1D02N/s1600/image.png" /></a></div><br /><p></p><p>In the 1800s, the British empire expanded by conquering kingdoms, imposing restrictive policies, and controlling trade. Yet the empire’s most effective and enduring method of oppression unfolded in classrooms and minds of their colonial subjects rather than on battlefields or in administrative offices. In 1835, British politician Thomas Babbington Macaulay, a key architect of colonial education, published Minute on Indian Education, in which he argued, “We must…form a class who may be interpreters between us and the millions whom we govern; a class of persons, Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals, and intellect” (Macaulay). The rest of Macaulay’s document proceeded to lay down the foundations of Macaulayism, the education system the British introduced to India. Macaulayism prioritized the English language over native languages, suppressed native culture, and emphasized rote memorization over critical thinking, reflecting the British view that the Indian population needed to have English tastes. Through these priorities, Macaulayism acted as a means of division and oppression of the Indian people. This result may seem paradoxical: Education is conventionally associated with equality, knowledge, and innovation. However, Macaulay’s vision excluded the creation of Indian thinkers, builders, and leaders who could challenge British rule. Instead, Macaulay sought to create interpreters--clerks and laborers who followed British orders; he wanted to create an Indian population that would facilitate the consolidation and maintenance of the vast British empire. Therefore, instead of acting as a means of positive development, the education system introduced by the British increased social inequality within India, stripped Indians of their civic understanding, and reduced the population’s ability to innovate (Vishnu 1). The repercussions of such an education system went beyond British India: Even today, seventy years after India has gained independence from the British crown, instruction in English and methods of “cramming” continue to dominate the Indian education system, hindering individual critical thinking and suppressing native culture.&nbsp;</p><p>The education system in India illustrates a widespread practice of privileged groups creating and maintaining oppressive education systems to consolidate power. These groups establish such systems by restricting the range of topics taught in school and limiting educational methods through which students are allowed to learn. These constraints suppress native culture, inhibit independent thought, and contribute to poverty cycles-- conditions that preserve dominance of those already in power. In other words, power structures with unchecked privilege, though framed as unavoidable in society, maintain intentionally restrictive education systems. By design, these systems limit individual freedoms and consequently hinder societal development.</p><p>Colonization presents a prime example of a power structure with unchecked privilege:&nbsp; the colonizer’s privilege enables them to create a restrictive education system that subjugates native communities. Similar to British Macaulayism in India, many imperial powers historically engineered colonial education with the expressed purpose of impeding the progress of the groups they oppressed. For example, a global empire in the 16th century, the Spanish colonized Paraguay in 1537 and ruled the country for three centuries. During their reign, the Spanish introduced a western education system and standardized education. Their changes painted an illusion of progress but careful analysis reveals its subtle undertones of oppression: the Spanish banned the indigenous language, Guarani, from being spoken in Paraguayan schools (going as far as to beat students for speaking the language), thereby suppressing native language and culture (Mccormick 1). Similar to Macaulayism in India, the colonial education system in Paraguay persisted beyond centuries of Spanish rule into the era of independent Paraguay. Remnants of colonial-era education continue to be reflected in Paraguayan law today. Sociology professor Moore, a researcher on Paraguayan education systems, pointed out, “Medical students at Universidad Nacional are not required to pass an exam in Guaraní as part of their training” (Moore 18). The Paraguayan government still allows students to think of Guarani as optional because of this mandate. Since many of these students went through the Paraguayan school system--which punished them for speaking Guarani--they continue to speak Spanish and deprioritize Paraguay’s native language. Subtle yet significant, this mandate perpetuates a colonial-era suppression of native culture. Besides restricting native language and culture, this mandate also oppresses Paraguay’s native people by cultivating a dangerous environment for the them: If they need medical attention, they will be unable to communicate with their doctor in their native language, potentially increasing their discomfort and medical risk--effectively forcing them to learn Spanish or to be treated as foreigners in their country. As a result, even though Paraguay is an independent country, the Paraguayan education system is still representative of lingering cultural colonization. Besides simply exemplifying an oppressive oppressive education system, the Paraguayan curriculum also reflects the manner in which such&nbsp; systems hinder society overall. Along with weakening cultural cohesion and identity in Paraguay, the preference for the Spanish language cultivates a language barrier for opportunities in the country. Many Guarani speakers in rural areas are treated as less educated, receive fewer opportunities, and even have less of a say in national policies. Although these disadvantages impact specific demographics significantly, they also impede progress in Paraguay as a whole by eliminating a portion of diverse thought and labor the country could have reaped from its population, limiting the country’s potential growth. As in India and in Paraguay, education also served as a tool for consolidating the power of privileged groups in Africa. Before the 15th century, this continent was home to some of the most resource-rich empires in the world such as Ghana and Mali. Over the next few centuries, European powers carved up these empires in the “scramble for Africa,” while colonizing and oppressing native populations (Kulik 1). Historian Walter Rodney famously argued that colonial schools produced “education for subordination, exploitation, [and] the development of underdevelopment,” a description cited by journalist Sasha Alyson in the Pan African Review (qtd. in Alyson 1). As Rodney indicates, the aim of colonial education was to foster obedient masses which would avoid independent development. To achieve this aim, privileged European powers emphasized rote memorization. Rodney’s observation that restrictive methods of schooling in Africa led to “education for subordination” aligns with philosopher Paolo Freire’s analysis in his book The Pedagogy of the Oppressed: “No oppressive order could permit the oppressed to begin to question: Why?” (Freire 11). Whereas Rodney provides historical details about African colonial education, Freire explains the mechanism by which the education proved oppressive: rote memorization prevented students from questioning authority and thereby ensured the continuation of subordination. In the interest of controlling the minds of a population, an “oppressive order” needed to avoid critical thinking and focus on other mechanical methods of education. Together, Rodney and Freire reveal that colonial education was designed more than just to control the content students learned; these colonial systems sought to control the manner in which students thought, maintaining oppression by preventing independent questioning. The oppressive impacts of these educational methods on African students are even more apparent in the present, as global organizations such as the United Nations shape African countries’ education policies. The United Nations has a pivotal role in African educational policies but little representation from the continent itself (most of its decision making board constitutes of the privileged global north), a fact that is reflected in the policies they pass: “When they [the United Nations] decided in 1990 to focus on education, these agencies set a goal of getting more children into schools that were remnants of the colonial era,” explained author Sasha Alyson in the article How Europe Underdeveloped Africa (Alyson 2). By focusing on enrollment as a sign of educational progress, global agencies are expanding colonial education in African countries. Expanding ineffective systems only worsens their inefficiency: there are only approximately 10.8% second graders reading at grade level in Nigeria (Alyson 1). An inefficient education system left over from colonial times is difficult to identify as the sole contributor to this dismal statistic. However, the system is still a significant contributor as evidenced by the fact that many students in the country actually seemed to do better in academics upon coming back to schools after the Covid pandemic (Alyson 1). Time spent in school with rote memorization as a daily practice contributed more negatively to literacy rates than time out of school which prompted students to think and question freely-- supporting Freire’s aforementioned point that critical thinking is essential to independent learning. If a majority of students in a country cannot read or think critically, can they solve problems that belong to larger systems (such as the nation)? Can they contribute to the national economy by innovating or running large corporations? Significant yet overlooked, the north’s dictation of the African education system is a subtle means of present-day colonization, one which follows similar to old-style colonization in its weaponization of education to hinder societal development. In conclusion, the Paraguayan education system is a product of lingering past colonization; African countries’ education system is a product of both past colonization and present-day colonization. In both regions of the world, education (ironically the process by which individuals are supposed to develop to then contribute to the community) is used as a means to oppress native culture and to restrict individual development, thus inhibiting societal progress.&nbsp;</p><p>Power structures that intentionally restrict education exist beyond third-world countries such as India, Paraguay, and Nigeria. A closer analysis reveals that these structures persist in the United States, a country at the global forefront of progress. One of the most extreme examples of unchecked power structures in American history was the relationship between slaveholders and slaves, in which slaveholders withheld essential forms of education from their slaves. Frederick Douglass, an African American famous for his abolitionist efforts, described in his autobiography the difficulty with which he learned to read and write. He needed to resort to learning letters off carved ships and asking questions of strangers under the guise of casual conversation (Douglass 3). This difficulty was a product of systemic oppression of slaves through withholding "unnecessary" forms of education. Despite these struggles, when Douglass finally learned to read, he said that he felt as if he had gained, “the power of truth over even the conscience of a slaveholder” (Douglass 2). In this moment, Douglass represented the ultimate fear of every slaveholder: he had broken free from the slaveholders’ restricted education system. Instead of solely learning skills necessary to labor on the farm such as farming and cooking (as the slaveholders wanted), Douglass had also learned to read, think critically, and ask questions. Douglass’s education had a ripple effect in history. Frederick Douglass, a former slave turned abolitionist, was a singular individual, but he was able to lobby for emancipation of African-Americans, was an invaluable part of the American Civil War effort, and became an icon in American history. Yet Douglass’s difficulties in gaining education illustrate that Douglass’s fate was a rare exception among other African Americans. Douglass’s status as an exception reveals the desolate manner in which limited education for African-Americans hindered society overall: There were many other African-Americans who could have vitally contributed to American society. However, slaveholders maintained restrictive education systems which entrapped many African-Americans in cycles of slavery and poverty, thus losing out on the potential of the American people. Power structures in America oppressing racial groups via education is more than some relic of the distant pre-Civil war era: even after emancipation, the restrictive education cycles persisted. In fact, African-American education was restricted first by segregation (as recently as 1964) and then red-lining (which many argue is ongoing). Another modern example further demonstrates unchecked privilege maintaining oppressive education in favor of consolidating power. In a popular political cartoon, American editorial cartoonist Mike Keefe depicts the 2009 budget cuts for education in the United States. Keefe presents the recession-era schoolchildren as “the ones who must be let go,” a label that exposes how budget priorities discarded students while protecting corporate interests (Keefe). Schools shutting down and receiving less funding parallels Douglass’s struggles to learn. Students in areas with school budget cuts have less access to educational material and opportunities. At first glance, the connection between unchecked privilege and school closure is obscure to the viewer (especially since the government is supposed to be a body with checks and balances). However, one must consider the historical context: During the 2009 recession, the government was providing budgets for defense and cutting taxes for businesses due to private and corporate lobbying (. On the other hand, the government was cutting education which disproportionately affected young rural schoolchildren, due to rural districts’ vulnerability to budget cuts (Soulliere 1). Instead of the government, corporations and private investors with exorbitant amounts of money are the ones with “unchecked privilege” in this scenario: Their money allows them to mold policies into personal favors that optimize their own interests, while restricting education for the less privileged. How are these budget cuts oppressive and how do they hinder society? These budget cuts perpetuate poverty cycles in the country, since many of these budget cuts were disproportionately enacted in rural areas with poorer populations (as evidenced by the setting of Keefe’s cartoon). Therefore, these schoolchildren will receive limited education, will likely be&nbsp; excluded from higher-salary jobs and trapped on lower rungs of the socioeconomic ladder. Although different from previous examples of education enforcing cultural oppression, the scenario of the 2009 budget cuts cultivates another kind of oppression-- economic oppression. Economic oppression of the majority harms American society as a whole by: maintaining unequal wealth distribution, silencing diverse perspectives in policy-creation (which would only reflect the elites’ perspectives), and undermining the promise of social mobility represented by unifying ideas such as the American Dream. Clearly, power structures that oppress through education are present beyond the countries many think of as “developing” and are also present in modern and progressive countries like the United States.&nbsp;</p><p>Power structures with the ability to hinder societal progress by maintaining oppressive educational structures may seem inevitable. However, increasing accessibility—defined here as equitable access to resources, quality instruction, and opportunities for critical thinking—empowers historically marginalized groups to lead educational reform, mitigating the effects of oppressive structures. This approach is supported by the aforementioned research paper about oppressive educational systems in Paraguay; sociology professor Moore suggested that if education was made increasingly accessible to historically oppressed groups, they would be able to participate in civic affairs and hold leadership positions within society by enabling social mobility. Without an educated and diverse group leading policy reform efforts, power would continue to only be consolidated by the privileged; subsequently, education would continue to only be shaped by the privileged. Limiting policy-making to elites would lead to a “reform disconnect,” which Moore defined as, “governments executing policy failing to align with the needs of their constituents” (Moore 4). Moore’s reasoning is sound: if one is privileged, they might find empathizing with and understanding the difficulties of an unprivileged life a challenging task. An exemplification of reform disconnect is the aforementioned occurrence of United Nations shaping education policy for African countries. According to Alyson in The Pan African Review, “Of the 74 agencies, funds, and programs under the UN umbrella, 67 are based in the Global North, though it has only a quarter of the globe’s population” (Alyson 1). Although the United Nations fails to be recognized as an independent “government” the organization still enacts policies which affect regions worldwide. The fact that the organization doesn’t have representation from around the world limits its perspective and incentives in policy-making for certain global areas. If more people from marginalized groups had access to education and subsequently led educational reform in the United Nations, the organization would be able to diversify and gain new perspectives on educational systems better suited for certain regions of the world. This diversity in perspective would aid in repurposing previously oppressive education systems into structures aligned with local needs and progress. In fact, effective examples of this kind of “bottom-up” reform include: North American indigenous leaders reviving the Anishinaabemowin language through community-led language acquisition programs, the Brazilian Land Workers Movement incorporating local farming practices into public school curriculum, and schools influenced by the Zapatista movement in Mexico (another movement of rural populations) enabling students to serve their communities as part of their education (Moore 20). As demonstrated by the above examples, reforms led by marginalized groups are able to accurately reflect local skill needs and preserve local culture and customs-- starkly differing from the homogenizing colonial education systems which aimed to serve foreign entities. By increasing the relevancy and inclusion of local education, such reforms increase the amount of productive workforce and accelerate societal progress.</p><p>Throughout history and across the globe, those with extreme amounts of privilege over another group have consolidated their power by maintaining oppressive education systems-- systems that disrupt societal progress as a whole. Although unequal power structures seem inevitable, their negative impacts are mitigated by expanding unrestricted education for marginalized groups. Today, many question programs encouraging diversity and inclusion of marginalized groups by asking, “Should I support these programs if they do not support me?” In reality, these programs correct systemic imbalances and allow the community to fully benefit from the talents and perspectives of all its members. Therefore, citizens of the world should instead question, “How can I contribute to these programs as they support my community?” Throughout this essay the “privileged” have been viewed as contributors to the problem, whereas in the current world--in which most of us hold some sort of privilege-- we need to be contributors to the solution. We need to question our assumptions, empathize with diverse challenges, and embrace new cultures, so that we can empower society, foster growth, and reclaim education as a tool for liberation.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;</p><br /><br /><br /><br /><br /><br /><br /><p>Works Cited</p><p>Alyson, Sasha. “Colonial Education Remains Alive and Well.” Pan African Review, 21 March 2022, https://panafricanreview.com/colonial-education-remains-alive-and-well/.
